From GSoC aspirant to founder

By Arush Kumar · August 2026 · 5 min read

Before Arush Labs had a name, I was submitting pull requests nobody had asked for, to codebases I didn't own, hoping a maintainer would look at my diff and say "yes, ship it." That's most of what GSoC aspiration actually is — not a program, but a habit of proposing real fixes to real projects and living with the review process.

As a GSoC 2026 aspirant with the OWASP Foundation and C2SI, I sent three pull requests into WebGoat/WebiU — including a schema-aware sanitization fix and documentation cleanup — and built new vulnerability labs and features for PyGoat, a deliberately-insecure app used to teach developers how web security actually breaks. I also proposed project ideas built on NestJS, Redis, and Django, most of which never got picked up. That's fine. The proposals that don't land still teach you how to scope something a stranger can review in twenty minutes.

What a maintainer's "no" actually teaches you

Open source is the fastest feedback loop I've found for one specific skill: writing something small enough, and clear enough, that someone with zero context can evaluate it fast. A maintainer doesn't have time to guess what you meant. Neither does a client. That's the same discipline.

A pull request that gets merged on the first review taught me more about scoping work than any course did.

From labs to hackathons to a company

The same instinct — build the smallest real thing, get it in front of someone qualified to judge it — is what took me through the Cybersecurity Solution Track at HACK IITK 2026, hosted by C3iHub at IIT Kanpur, and the Microsoft Hackathon. Both were two-day sprints where the only thing that mattered was whether the thing worked when someone else tried to break it.

That's directly why Arush Labs' security products — SecureAI Agent, ThreatShield AI, and the tooling we're building around face verification and PR risk scoring — exist as products, not afterthoughts. I spent a year finding vulnerabilities in other people's code before I ever tried to ship anything of my own. It felt only right to build the AI-first company around not repeating those same mistakes.

If you're where I was

If you're a student sending PRs into projects that will probably ignore you: keep going. The rejections are the curriculum. The one PR that gets merged will teach you more about writing production-ready code than a semester of assignments — it certainly did for me.
